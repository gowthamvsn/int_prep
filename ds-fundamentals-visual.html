1 · Gradient Descent

The single mechanism nearly every model-fitting procedure in this guide reduces to: nudge the parameters a little in the direction that makes the loss smaller, repeat.

θ := θ − α · ∇L(θ)

θ is the parameter being learned, ∇L(θ) is the gradient of the loss with respect to it (which way is "uphill"), and α is the learning rate — how big a step to take each time. Below, the exact same loss surface L(θ)=(θ−5)² (minimum at θ=5), the exact same starting point (θ=−3), and the exact same 6 steps — only α changes. Every number is a real computed value from that recurrence, not illustrative.

The middle panel is the one worth internalizing: a well-chosen learning rate isn't the fastest theoretically possible descent, it's the one that actually converges — too small and you're technically doing gradient descent but running out of patience (or compute budget) before it matters; too large and each step doesn't just fail to help, it actively overshoots the minimum by more than the previous step did, so the loss grows, unboundedly, every single time. This is why learning-rate schedules (start larger, shrink over time) exist: get the speed of a large α early, the stability of a small α once you're close.

Where this shows upEvery neural-network optimizer (SGD, Adam, AdamW — all of them) is gradient descent with a different rule for choosing/adapting α and for how much of the past gradient to remember. If a training loss curve is flat, α is probably too small; if it's spiking or NaN-ing, α is probably too large — the three panels above are literally what those two failure modes look like on a loss curve, just isolated down to one parameter so the mechanism is visible.

2 · Loss Functions — MSE, Cross-Entropy & Huber

The loss function is what "wrong" means to the optimizer above — its shape decides which mistakes get punished hardest, which is why picking the right one for the task (regression vs. classification vs. outlier-prone regression) matters as much as picking the right model.

MSE = (1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² BCE = −[ y·ln(p) + (1−y)·ln(1−p) ] Huber = 0.5·e² if |e| ≤ δ = δ·(|e| − 0.5·δ) otherwise (e = y − ŷ)

All three panels plot real evaluations of their formula, not sketches. MSE (left) is symmetric and quadratic — being off by 5 costs 25, being off by 2 costs 4, a little over 6× the penalty for a little over 2× the error, which is exactly what "squared" means. Cross-entropy (middle) is built for probabilities specifically: predicting p=0.99 when the true label is 1 costs almost nothing (0.010); predicting p=0.01 — confidently and wrong — costs 4.605, over 400× as much, because −ln(p) heads toward infinity as a confident prediction turns out to be false. Huber (right) is what you reach for when your regression targets have real outliers: at a normal-sized error (e=1.5) it behaves almost like MSE (1.00 vs. MSE's 2.25), but at a genuine outlier (e=8) it charges only 7.5 where MSE would charge 64 — beyond the threshold δ, the penalty switches from quadratic to linear, so one bad outlier can't dominate the whole training loss the way it would under pure MSE.

Where this shows upRegression tasks (predict a number: price, ETA, temperature) default to MSE, or Huber specifically when the labels are known to be noisy/outlier-prone (sensor glitches, fat-fingered data entry). Classification tasks (predict a class or probability) default to cross-entropy — and this is also the same shape of idea behind an LLM's training loss (next-token cross-entropy) and behind perplexity, which is just e^(average cross-entropy).

3 · Optimizers — SGD vs. Momentum vs. Adam

Gradient descent (§1) says "step downhill"; an optimizer is the specific rule for how big a step and in what adjusted direction. All three below take the exact same 16 steps from the exact same starting point on the exact same loss surface — a real, computed "ravine" (steep in one direction, shallow in the other, the shape that makes optimizer choice actually matter) — only the update rule changes.

SGD: w := w − α·∇L(w) Momentum: v := β·v + ∇L(w); w := w − α·v Adam: m := β₁·m + (1−β₁)·∇L(w) (momentum on the gradient) v := β₂·v + (1−β₂)·∇L(w)² (momentum on the squared gradient) w := w − α · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) (per-parameter adaptive step size)

Real, computed trajectories on L(x,y)=10x²+y² from (x,y)=(−3.5,−3.0): vanilla SGD zigzags in the steep x-direction (crossing zero 6 times) while crawling in the shallow y-direction, ending at loss 0.151. Momentum overshoots even further at first (x swings to +1.25, more than SGD's +0.7) because it's carrying velocity from previous steps — but that same velocity carries it through the shallow direction faster, ending at the lowest loss of the three, 0.039. Adam's path is smooth in both directions at once, zero sign flips anywhere, because it rescales each parameter's step by its own gradient history — no hand-tuning of a separate learning rate per direction required — ending at 0.119, respectable without any manual tuning at all.

Where this shows upAdamW (Adam + a decoupled weight-decay term) is the default optimizer for essentially every modern transformer/LLM training run. Plain SGD (often with momentum) is still common in classic computer-vision training, where its slightly worse convergence speed is traded for better final generalization in some regimes — a real, debated tradeoff, not a strict "Adam always wins."

4 · The Training Pipeline — Init → Data → Compile → Train → Evaluate

Sections 1–3 each isolated one mechanism (the update rule, what "wrong" means, how the step is adapted). This section is the scaffolding all three sit inside — the same five stages, in the same order, whether the model is a tiny logistic regression, a ResNet, or a transformer. Only the specific pieces plugged into each stage change.

1. Initialize — build the model, random starting weights 2. Data — Dataset + DataLoader: shuffle, batch, feed 3. Compile — attach a loss function + an optimizer (+ metrics to watch) 4. Train — for each epoch: for each batch: forward → loss → backward → step → zero_grad 5. Evaluate — eval mode, no gradient tracking, forward pass only (inference)

"Compile" is literally Keras's word for stage 3 — model.compile(optimizer=..., loss=..., metrics=...) attaches those three pieces before model.fit() can run. PyTorch has no equivalent call — you just construct criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss() and optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), ...) as plain objects, then reference them yourself inside the loop you write. (Careful: PyTorch 2.x also has a function literally named torch.compile() — that's an unrelated feature, a JIT/graph optimizer that makes an existing model run faster, not a stage-3 wiring step. Same word, two different meanings, one framework.)

### PyTorch — you write the loop yourself model = torchvision.models.resnet18(num_classes=10) train_loader = DataLoader(train_dataset, batch_size=8, shuffle=True) criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss() # stage 3: loss optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3) # stage 3: optimizer for epoch in range(n_epochs): # stage 4: TRAIN model.train() for xb, yb in train_loader: # loop over batches optimizer.zero_grad() # clear old gradients logits = model(xb) # forward pass loss = criterion(logits, yb) # compute loss loss.backward() # backward pass (autograd) optimizer.step() # update weights model.eval() # stage 5: EVALUATE / INFERENCE with torch.no_grad(): # no autograd bookkeeping needed preds = model(x_test).argmax(dim=1) ### Keras — the same 5 stages, framework does the loop for you model = keras.applications.ResNet18(...) # stage 1: init model.compile(optimizer="adamw", loss="sparse_categorical_crossentropy", metrics=["accuracy"]) # stage 3: compile model.fit(x_train, y_train, epochs=n_epochs, batch_size=8) # stage 2+4: data + train preds = model.predict(x_test) # stage 5: evaluate/inference

Real run, not illustrative: 32 samples, batch_size=8 → 4 batches per epoch, an Adam-updated logistic regression with binary cross-entropy loss, trained for 8 real epochs. Loss falls 0.6438 → 0.3707, monotonically, ending at 90.6% training accuracy — this is stage 4 of the pipeline above, concretely, with an actual epoch axis and an actual per-epoch loss number, the same quantity a real training log prints every epoch.

Where this shows up"Epoch" = one full pass over every batch in the dataset. "Batch size" = how many samples get averaged into one gradient/update. Small batches → noisier but more frequent updates (more steps per epoch); large batches → smoother but fewer updates per epoch, and a much bigger memory footprint (see the KV-cache/QLoRA memory math elsewhere in this guide for why memory is the usual limiter). DataLoader(shuffle=True) reshuffles the batch order every epoch specifically so the model doesn't learn a spurious pattern tied to sample order.

Optimizer options — Adam vs. AdamW, the decoupled-decay difference

§3 already showed SGD/Momentum/Adam differ in how they use gradient history. AdamW differs from Adam in something else entirely: where weight decay (L2-style shrinkage toward zero, used to fight overfitting) gets applied. Plain "Adam + L2" folds the decay into the gradient before Adam's per-parameter 1/√v̂ rescaling — so the SAME nominal decay ends up shrinking different parameters by very different real amounts, depending on each one's own gradient history. AdamW applies decay directly, outside that rescaling.

Real recurrence, both starting at w=1.0, both given the identical nominal weight_decay=0.1, 5 steps, zero task-gradient (isolating decay alone): a parameter with a small gradient-history (small v̂) shrinks to 0.5092 under Adam+L2 but a parameter with a large gradient-history (large v̂) only shrinks to 0.8466 — a real 0.337 gap between two parameters given the exact same decay setting. Under AdamW, both shrink to exactly 0.9510 — a 0.000 gap. This unpredictable coupling is the specific, published reason (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) AdamW replaced Adam+L2 as the default for transformer/LLM training.

Loss options — Binary vs. Categorical Cross-Entropy

§2 covered BCE for a 2-class (yes/no) output. Most real classifiers — including a ResNet trained on 10 or 1000 classes — output a probability over many classes at once, via softmax, and use Categorical Cross-Entropy (CCE): the exact same −ln(p) shape as BCE, just applied to the probability the softmax assigned the one correct class.

CCE = −ln( p_true_class ) where p = softmax(logits)

Real 3-class softmax, logits [2.0, 0.5, −1.0], true class = index 0: softmax gives that class 78.56% probability, so CCE = 0.2413 — cheap, because the model was right. Flip the logits to [−1.0, 0.5, 2.0] (same true class, now confidently predicted wrong): that class's probability collapses to 3.91%, and CCE jumps over 13× higher to 3.2413 — exactly the same "confidently wrong costs enormously more" shape BCE showed in §2, just extended across more than 2 outcomes.

Where this shows upnn.BCELoss / "binary_crossentropy" → single output neuron + sigmoid, 2 classes (or independent multi-label flags). nn.CrossEntropyLoss / "sparse_categorical_crossentropy" → one output neuron per class + softmax, exactly one correct class — this is what a ResNet classifying 1000 ImageNet categories, or an LLM predicting the next token over its whole vocabulary, actually trains against. PyTorch's nn.CrossEntropyLoss is a genuine convenience, not a separate algorithm — it fuses softmax + CCE into one numerically-stable call, which is why real PyTorch code passes raw logits into it directly rather than pre-computing softmax first.

5 · Bias-Variance Tradeoff & Overfitting/Underfitting

One curve explains both ideas at once, because they're the same phenomenon looked at from two angles.

Total Error ≈ Bias² + Variance + Irreducible Noise

Bias is error from a model too simple to capture the real pattern — it makes the same kind of mistake consistently, on training data and new data alike, which is why the underfitting zone (left) has both curves high and close together. Variance is error from a model so flexible it starts fitting noise specific to the training set — it memorizes quirks that don't generalize, which is why the overfitting zone (right) shows training error still falling while test error climbs back up: the model is getting better at the training set specifically, not better in general. The sweet spot isn't "as complex as possible" or "as simple as possible" — it's the point where the two failure modes cross over, and it's found empirically (via a validation curve exactly like this one), not by formula.

Where this shows upThis is the entire justification for a held-out validation set: training loss alone can't distinguish "learning the real pattern" from "memorizing the training set" — only error on data the model never trained on can, which is exactly the gap plotted here.

6 · L1 vs. L2 Regularization

Both penalize large weights to fight the overfitting/high-variance side of the tradeoff above — the difference is how they penalize, and that difference has a real geometric consequence, verified below by actually solving both constrained optimization problems (grid search over the boundary), not asserted.

L1 (Lasso): minimize Loss(w) + λ Σ|wᵢ| ⟺ constrain Σ|wᵢ| ≤ t L2 (Ridge): minimize Loss(w) + λ Σwᵢ² ⟺ constrain Σwᵢ² ≤ t²

Same loss surface (elliptical contours centered on the true unconstrained optimum, w=(0.3, 2.0)), same constraint budget (t=0.9) — two different constraint shapes, two different real solutions. L1's diamond has corners sitting exactly on the axes; the closest point on that diamond to the true optimum turns out to be one of those corners, computed here as exactly (0, 0.9) — w₁ isn't nearly zero, it's exactly zero. L2's circle has no corners anywhere, so its closest boundary point is a generic point on the curve, computed as (0.18, 0.88) — both weights shrink toward zero, but neither one reaches it. This is the literal mechanism behind "L1 does feature selection, L2 just shrinks" — not a rule of thumb, a direct consequence of one shape having corners and the other not.

Where this shows upL1 (Lasso) is the right call when you suspect most features are irrelevant and want the model to zero them out automatically. L2 (Ridge) is the right call when features are correlated and you'd rather shrink them all a bit than arbitrarily pick which one survives. Elastic Net (both penalties, weighted) exists specifically because real problems often want a bit of each.

7 · Hyperparameters — Classical ML, Boosting & LLMs

Everything above (loss, optimizer, regularization strength) is controlled by numbers you set before training starts, not learned from data — that's what "hyper" means. Three real examples, one from each world, each with an actual before/after result, not a definition.

Classical ML — k in k-Nearest-Neighbors

Real dataset (scikit-learn make_moons, 300 points, 30% held out as test), real KNeighborsClassifier runs at three values of k.

k=1 memorizes the training set exactly (100% train accuracy) but generalizes worst of the three (86.7% test) — classic overfitting, each prediction driven by a single, possibly-noisy neighbor. k=9 is the real sweet spot found by this exact sweep: lower train accuracy (90.5%) than k=1, but the best test accuracy of any k tried (94.4%). k=150 — half the training set — underfits: both train (78.1%) and test (80.0%) accuracy drop and sit close together, the bias-variance signature from §4 showing up again, this time on one concrete, real hyperparameter.

Advanced ML — learning_rate in Gradient Boosting

Real dataset (scikit-learn make_regression, 400 samples, 10 features, real noise), real GradientBoostingRegressor with n_estimators=100 held fixed while learning_rate varies.

learning_rate=0.01, too small for only 100 trees: the ensemble hasn't converged yet, so both train (R²=0.636) and test (R²=0.511) are mediocre — this is underfitting from a learning-rate/n_estimators mismatch, not from the model being too simple. learning_rate=0.1 is the sweet spot at this tree count: train R²=0.993, test R²=0.870, the best test score of the three. learning_rate=1.0 overshoots: the ensemble fits the training set perfectly (R²=1.000) but test R² collapses to 0.661 — each tree is now correcting the full residual instead of a damped fraction of it, so early trees overfit hard and later trees can't recover.

LLMs — Temperature

Real softmax computed at three temperatures over the same 5 next-token logits (a toy but real example: completing "The capital of France is").

At T=0.5, "Paris" takes essentially all the probability mass (99.97%) — near-deterministic, low-diversity output. At T=1.0 (the logits' natural scale), "Paris" still dominates (97.19%) but the other tokens have real, nonzero probability. At T=2.0, "Paris" drops to 75.44% and every other token gains meaningfully — entropy (a real measure of "how spread out" a distribution is) rises from 0.003 nats at T=0.5 to 0.887 nats at T=2.0, over 300× higher. Temperature doesn't change which token the model thinks is most likely — the logits are identical in all three panels — it only changes how much probability mass gets shared with the runners-up.

Where this shows upThe pattern repeats across all three: there's rarely a hyperparameter value that's "correct" in the abstract, only one that's correct for a specific dataset/model-size/budget, found empirically — exactly the same lesson as the learning-rate panels in §1 and the model-complexity curve in §4, just wearing a different hyperparameter's name each time.

8 · Practice Q&A — Self-Test

Ten recall questions covering every topic above, grounded in the exact real numbers already computed and shown in the diagrams — no new claims, just active-recall practice.

Q1. In the three-panel gradient descent diagram, why did the "too big" learning rate (α=1.05) cause the loss to GROW from 64 to 201 instead of just converging slowly like the "too small" case?

A: The update coefficient (1−2α) exceeded 1 in absolute value (|1−2.1|=1.1>1) for this quadratic loss, so each step overshot the minimum by more than the previous distance — a true divergence, not slowness. Loss went 64→77→94→113→137→166→201, growing every single step.

Q2. What was the exact recurrence used to compute every gradient descent step, and why does it matter that these numbers were computed rather than sketched?

A: θ := θ − α·∇L(θ) on L(θ)=(θ−5)², run for real for 6 steps from θ=−3 at three learning rates. Because the numbers are actually computed, the diagram can show a genuine divergence at α=1.05 rather than an idealized picture that might accidentally look plausible either way.

Q3. At the outlier error e=8, what's the exact numeric gap between MSE and Huber loss (δ=1), and why does that gap exist?

A: MSE(8)=64, Huber(8)=7.5 — an 8.5× difference. Beyond the threshold δ=1, Huber's penalty switches from quadratic (0.5e²) to linear (δ·(|e|−0.5δ)), so one large outlier can't dominate the total loss the way it would under pure MSE.

Q4. Why does binary cross-entropy for a true label y=1 approach infinity as predicted probability p→0, and what real numbers demonstrate that?

A: Because BCE = −ln(p) for y=1, and −ln(p)→∞ as p→0. Real computed values: p=0.99 costs 0.010, p=0.01 costs 4.605 — over 400× more, even though the raw prediction only moved 0.98 in probability space.

Q5. In the SGD vs. Momentum vs. Adam diagram, why did Momentum overshoot even FURTHER than plain SGD (x swinging to +1.25 vs. SGD's +0.7) and still end up with the lowest final loss?

A: Momentum accumulates velocity from past gradients (v:=βv+∇L(w)), so it can overshoot more in high-curvature directions early on — but that same accumulated velocity carries it through the shallow direction faster, giving it the lowest final loss (0.039) of the three after 16 steps, versus SGD's 0.151.

Q6. Why did Adam's trajectory show zero sign flips in either coordinate, unlike SGD's visible zigzag in the steep direction?

A: Adam rescales each parameter's effective step size using its own running gradient-magnitude history (the second-moment estimate v̂), automatically normalizing the steep and shallow directions without any manual per-direction learning-rate tuning — eliminating the zigzag SGD shows on the identical ravine-shaped loss surface. Final loss: 0.119, no oscillation anywhere in the path.

Q7. In the model-complexity curve, what specifically defines the "sweet spot," and why isn't it just "as complex as possible"?

A: The sweet spot (complexity≈4 in the diagram) is where test/validation error hits its minimum (0.382) — even though training error keeps falling past that point, down to 0.016 at complexity=10. Past the sweet spot the model is fitting training-set noise (variance), not learning more of the real pattern, so test error climbs back up even as training error keeps improving.

Q8. What real, computed values proved that L1 produces a sparse solution while L2 doesn't, on the same loss surface and same budget?

A: With loss=2(w1−0.3)²+(w2−2.0)² and budget t=0.9, a grid search found the L1 diamond's closest boundary point at exactly (0, 0.9) — w1 exactly zero — while the L2 circle's closest point was (0.183, 0.881) — both weights nonzero. This was verified by numeric optimization, not just asserted from the textbook rule.

Q9. In the real kNN sweep, why did k=1 get the WORST test accuracy despite getting the BEST (100%) training accuracy?

A: k=1 lets a single, possibly-noisy neighbor decide every prediction, so it memorizes the training set exactly (100% train accuracy) but that memorization doesn't generalize — test accuracy was only 86.7%, worse than k=9's 94.4%, which averages over more neighbors and smooths out individual noisy points.

Q10. Why did the Gradient Boosting run with learning_rate=1.0 achieve perfect training R² (1.000) but the worst test R² (0.661) of the three rates tried?

A: At learning_rate=1.0, each new tree corrects the FULL residual instead of a damped fraction of it, so early trees overfit hard to training-set noise and later trees can't recover — the same overshoot-and-overfit failure mode as too large a learning rate in plain gradient descent, just expressed through an ensemble instead of a single parameter update.

Q11. In the real 8-epoch training run, what specifically does "one epoch" correspond to given batch_size=8 and 32 total samples?

A: One epoch = one full pass over every sample in the dataset, split into batches. With 32 samples and batch_size=8, that's exactly 4 batches per epoch — each epoch's reported loss (e.g. 0.6438 for epoch 1) is the average BCE loss across those 4 batches, not a single batch's loss.

Q12. Why did the small-gradient-history parameter shrink MORE under Adam+L2 (to 0.5092) than under AdamW (to 0.9510), given the identical nominal weight_decay=0.1 in both?

A: Adam+L2 divides the decay term by that parameter's own √v̂ (its second-moment gradient history) as part of Adam's normal adaptive-step-size math. A small v̂ means dividing by a small number, which amplifies the effective decay far beyond the nominal 0.1 — a real, computed side effect, not a labeling difference. AdamW never divides the decay term by anything, so it always shrinks by the same proportional amount regardless of that parameter's gradient history.

Q13. In the categorical cross-entropy example, both panels used logits of the exact same three magnitudes (2.0, 0.5, -1.0) — why did reordering which class got which logit change the loss by over 13×?

A: CCE only cares about the probability mass softmax assigns to the TRUE class, not the raw logit magnitudes themselves. Assigning the true class the highest logit gives it 78.56% probability (CCE=0.2413); assigning that same true class the lowest logit gives it only 3.91% probability (CCE=3.2413) — same three numbers, completely different loss, because what matters is which class each number ends up attached to.

Q14. Why does PyTorch's nn.CrossEntropyLoss expect raw logits as input instead of pre-computed softmax probabilities?

A: It fuses the softmax and the −ln(p) cross-entropy computation into one numerically-stable operation internally (avoiding computing exp() of large numbers and then a separate log() that could lose precision). Passing in already-softmaxed probabilities and a separate loss would double-apply softmax-like squashing and produce a mathematically wrong gradient — the fused version is a correctness requirement, not just a convenience.
