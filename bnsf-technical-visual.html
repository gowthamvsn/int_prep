1 · Optimization Algorithms (LP / MILP)

BNSF's own screening questionnaire attaches a specific bar to this one — 6+ years building optimization algorithms — the only technical item on the form with an explicit years requirement. Linear programming (LP) optimizes a linear objective under linear constraints over continuous variables; mixed-integer programming (MILP) is the same problem with some variables forced to whole numbers, which looks like a small change but makes the problem NP-hard in the worst case. Both examples below are real, solver-verified (PuLP/CBC) numbers, not illustrative ones.

The geometry of an LP optimum — it's always a corner

Take a concrete allocation problem: two train configurations, type A and type B, to build this period. Each type-A train needs 2 crew-hours and 1 yard-slot; each type-B needs 1 crew-hour and 1 yard-slot. Crew hours are capped at 40, yard capacity at 30. Type A nets $40 profit, type B nets $30. Maximize profit.

maximize 40x + 30y
subject to 2x + y ≤ 40 (crew hours)
          x + y ≤ 30 (yard capacity)
          x, y ≥ 0

The reason LP is solvable in polynomial time in practice (simplex) despite an enormous space of possible (x,y) pairs: the optimum of a linear objective over a convex feasible region is always at a vertex — never floating in the interior of a face, never strictly inside the region. Simplex exploits this directly: instead of scanning the whole shaded area, it walks vertex to vertex along the boundary, always improving, until no neighboring vertex is better. Here, that walk finds (x=10, y=20), profit=$1000 — solver-verified, and visibly the last vertex the profit line touches before leaving the feasible region.

MILP — the same problem, with a bar it can't split

Change the numbers slightly (crew-hour coefficient 2→4 on one constraint, objective 40x+30y→5x+4y) so the LP relaxation's optimum lands on a genuinely fractional vertex — real solver output, not a contrived textbook fraction:

maximize 5x + 4y
subject to 2x + 3y ≤ 34
          4x + 3y ≤ 48
          x, y ≥ 0, x and y must be whole trains

You can't dispatch 6.667 trains, so the LP relaxation's answer (x=7, y=6.667, profit=$61.667) isn't a valid plan — it's an upper bound, and the whole branch-and-bound algorithm is a search for the best integer point that never exceeds it. Branch: pick the fractional variable (y=6.667) and split into two subproblems, y≤6 and y≥7 — every integer solution lives in exactly one of these, none are lost. Bound: solve each relaxed subproblem; if its optimum is already integer, it's a candidate answer (an “incumbent”); if a subproblem's relaxed bound is already worse than the best integer answer found so far, prune the whole subtree without solving it further — this pruning is what keeps branch-and-bound from degenerating into brute-force enumeration of every integer point.

Where I'd actually use this at BNSFTrain blocking (which railcars group into which train to minimize re-switching), crew scheduling under hours-of-service rules, and locomotive assignment are all realistic MILP formulations — decision variables per car/crew/locomotive, capacity and regulatory constraints, an objective in cost or cars-delayed. The 2-variable example above is deliberately small enough to see the mechanism; a real formulation might have thousands of variables, solved with the same branch-and-bound idea, just with much smarter branching/cutting-plane heuristics than exhaustive search.

2 · Geospatial Analytics & Route Optimization

Named explicitly in BNSF's screening questionnaire: “knowledge of geospatial analytics, route optimization, and GIS concepts.” Three distinct ideas get conflated under that heading — distance-on-a-sphere, shortest-path search, and multi-stop routing — each verified below with real computed numbers, not textbook placeholders.

Why a ruler on a lat/long map lies to you

Chicago to Los Angeles — a real BNSF transcontinental corridor — is 2,804 km by great-circle (Haversine) distance. Treat latitude and longitude as if they were flat x/y coordinates on a ruler (a very common first-pass mistake), and you get 3,507 km — a 25% overestimate. The reason isn't rounding error: a degree of longitude is a real physical distance only at the equator (111.3 km); by Chicago's latitude (41.9°N) it has already shrunk to 82.9 km, because longitude lines converge toward the poles while latitude lines stay parallel. Haversine accounts for this curvature directly; naive Euclidean distance on raw coordinates cannot.

Dijkstra vs. A* — same answer, very different amount of work

Both algorithms are guaranteed to find the same shortest path — 8.0 units on this real 15-node network, source to goal. The difference is how much of the graph they touch to get there. Dijkstra expands strictly by cumulative distance from the source, with no notion of where the goal is — so it explores the three decoy branches (small detours that go nowhere useful) before it's confident they're not shortcuts, expanding 14 of 15 nodes. A* adds a heuristic (straight-line distance to the goal) that makes it prioritize nodes that seem to be heading the right direction — it never touches a single decoy, expanding exactly 9 nodes, the direct path only. On a real, sparse rail network with genuine dead-end branches, that gap compounds fast.

TSP vs. VRP — the constraint that changes everything

Same 5 stops, same depot, same distance matrix — two different problems. TSP (one vehicle, visit everyone, return to depot) finds a single tour of 223 distance-units. The moment each stop has a demand (a real shipment size) and each vehicle has a real capacity limit, that single-tour answer may not even be feasible — here, total demand (17 units) exceeds one vehicle's capacity (9), so it's not optional to split the work. The capacity-respecting VRP answer uses 2 vehicles, 303 distance-units total — genuinely more total distance than the capacity-blind TSP number, and that's not a worse answer, it's the actual best feasible one. Reaching for a bare TSP formulation on a real capacity-constrained routing problem produces a number that looks better and describes a plan that can't be executed.

Where I'd actually use this at BNSFHaversine (or a proper projected coordinate system) underlies any distance/ETA calculation across the network. Dijkstra/A*-style search underlies dispatch and routing decision support at the track-segment level. TSP-vs-VRP is the exact shape of real fleet/crew routing — capacity, time windows, and hours-of-service rules are what make it a VRP, not a toy TSP, and OR-Tools (§ optimization above) is the standard tool for solving it at real scale.

3 · Vector Databases & Embeddings

Item 17 on BNSF's screening form, by name. Three mechanics worth being able to draw from memory: what an embedding actually captures, why nearest-neighbor search needs to be approximate at real scale, and how chunk boundaries can silently break retrieval.

What similarity actually looks like, with a real model

Four real sentences, embedded with a real pretrained model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 384 dimensions) — not hand-picked toy numbers. The two brake-wear sentences, worded completely differently, score 0.792 cosine similarity; every pairing against the unrelated revenue-report and crew-scheduling sentences scores near zero, including one slightly negative pairing. Nobody told the model these two sentences were about the same topic — that clustering fell out entirely from how the model was trained, which is exactly why embeddings power semantic search where keyword overlap alone would miss the paraphrase.

Why nearest-neighbor search has to be approximate

Brute-force search compares a query against every stored vector — exact, and fine at small scale. At real corpus sizes it stops being fine: 10 million stored chunks means 10 million comparisons, every query. An approximate index (HNSW is the common one) organizes vectors into a navigable graph so a query only has to hop through roughly log(N) comparisons to find a very-likely-correct nearest neighbor — the trade is a small, tunable chance of missing the true single-best match in exchange for a search that stays fast as the corpus grows by orders of magnitude.

Where a fact can get lost — chunk boundaries

A real 27-word sentence, fixed-size chunked at 10 words with no overlap, splits the deadline (“92 days”, chunk 1) from its consequence (“must be | immediately removed…”, split across chunks 2 and 3) — a query asking what happens if the deadline is missed can retrieve a chunk with half the fact and never surface the other half. A 5-word overlap creates a chunk that spans the boundary and contains the complete clause. This is the exact same failure mode named in core-technical-depth.md's RAG pitfalls, now shown on real word-for-word text instead of asserted abstractly.

Where I'd actually use this at BNSFThe vector-DB choice (FAISS/pgvector/Pinecone/MongoDB Atlas) matters far less than getting chunking and embedding-model fit right for the actual document set — maintenance manuals, safety procedures, engineering specs — which is exactly the “build vs. buy” framing already in core-technical-depth.md's RAG section.

4 · RAG, Chain-of-Thought & LLM Evaluation Methodologies

Item 16 on BNSF's screening form bundles these three together. RAG's chunking/retrieval mechanics are covered above (§3) and in core-technical-depth.md; this section covers the other two with something stronger than a claim — a real, reproducible failure and a real judge verdict, both run live through the same Azure OpenAI deployment powering this guide's click-to-ask feature.

Chain-of-Thought — a real, reproducible failure it fixes

A genuine multi-step word problem, run through the same model three separate times at temperature 0 (fully deterministic): asked for only the final number, it returns 406 — wrong, every single time. Asked to show its work step by step first, it correctly gets 417, with every intermediate calculation traceable. Nothing about the underlying model changed between the two calls — only whether it was allowed to spend tokens on intermediate reasoning before committing to an answer. This is why CoT's benefit concentrates on genuinely multi-step problems and does nothing for a single-lookup question — there's no intermediate reasoning to externalize.

Multi-agent debate — FinSight's actual architecture

The pattern behind the CoT result generalizes: instead of one model reasoning silently, split the reasoning across roles that check each other. This is the literal architecture of FinSight, the multi-agent platform described in behavioral-partnership-star-stories.md and core-technical-depth.md — a Portfolio agent proposes, a Market agent challenges with current conditions, a Critic agent is specifically prompted to find what the other two missed, before anything reaches the user.

LLM-as-judge — a real faithfulness verdict

A real maintenance-bulletin context, a real generated answer that mixes two true claims with one fabricated one (a made-up axle-replacement rule), scored by a real LLM-as-judge call using the exact rubric from core-technical-depth.md. The judge didn't just say “mostly good” — it identified the specific unsupported sentence by name and scored it 2/5, exactly the granularity a real faithfulness pipeline needs to be useful rather than a vague pass/fail.

Where I'd actually use this at BNSFCoT is nearly free — always try it first on any multi-step reasoning task before reaching for something heavier. Multi-agent patterns cost real latency and API spend (see GPU/serving discussion elsewhere), so they're worth it specifically when a single pass is genuinely error-prone, not by default. LLM-as-judge faithfulness scoring is exactly what I'd want gating any RAG system before it reaches a maintenance crew or safety-adjacent decision, given the same reasoning already in system-design-prep.md's RAG design section.

5 · GPU Optimization

Item 19 on BNSF's screening form. Four techniques, each solving a different constraint, each shown here with exact arithmetic on the same concrete model — GPT-2 small's real dimensions (dmodel=768, 12 layers), already established in the NCA-GENL transformer walkthrough — not made-up round numbers.

Mixed precision — where the memory actually gets saved

A common misconception: mixed precision mainly shrinks parameter/optimizer memory. It doesn't — optimizer states (Adam's momentum and variance) are kept in fp32 regardless, for numerical stability (“master weights”). The real savings are in activations — every intermediate tensor kept around for the backward pass. For one concrete, batch=8, sequence=1024 forward pass through GPT-2 small's 12 layers: 288 MB of hidden-state activations in fp32, exactly 144 MB in fp16/bf16 — a precise halving, because it's the same numbers stored in half the bytes, nothing more mysterious than that.

Gradient accumulation — simulating a bigger batch than memory allows

If your GPU can only fit a micro-batch of 4 sequences, but training is more stable at batch 32: run 8 micro-batches of forward+backward without zeroing gradients between them, letting them sum, then take exactly one optimizer step. 4 × 8 = 32 — the same effective batch, at the cost of 8× the wall-clock steps to see the same number of sequences, not extra memory.

Gradient checkpointing — trading compute for memory

Instead of keeping all 12 layers' activations (288 MB), checkpoint only 4 evenly-spaced layers and recompute the other 8 during the backward pass by re-running the forward computation for that segment. Stored activation memory drops to 96 MB — a third — at the cost of re-running roughly a third of the forward pass again, which is exactly where the commonly-cited “20–30% more compute” figure for checkpointing comes from: it's not a rule of thumb pulled from nowhere, it's the direct consequence of how many layers you chose to recompute.

DDP — the same math, run on N GPUs at once

Each GPU holds a full model replica and a different shard of the data, computes gradients independently, then an all-reduce step averages gradients across every replica before any optimizer step runs — so all replicas apply the identical update and stay in sync, behaving as if trained on one big batch, computed in parallel. This is a collective operation: every rank must call it the same number of times, in the same order, or the whole group deadlocks waiting for a peer that never shows up — the single most common real DDP bug.

Where I'd actually use this at BNSFThese four compose, in this order of complexity: mixed precision essentially always on for anything GPU-trained; gradient accumulation the moment batch size is memory-constrained; gradient checkpointing only once activation memory specifically (not batch size) is the bottleneck; DDP only once single-GPU training is confirmed to actually be the limiting factor — reaching for multi-GPU before that is solving a problem you don't have yet.
